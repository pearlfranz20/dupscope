dupscope

Finding reused images in scientific figures

A tool that checks a paper's figures for image panels that appear more than once, even when rotated, mirrored, resized, cropped or re-toned, and writes a report for a person to review. It was measured on synthetic papers with known answers, and is set up to run on real papers retracted for image problems.

Why this matters

Duplicated images are a common reason papers in biology and neuroscience are corrected or retracted. Some reuse is an honest mistake, some is deliberate, and a little is legitimate. Spotting it by eye means comparing every image panel with every other one, which nobody does reliably across a paper with dozens of panels. Software can do the comparing, and a person can do the judging.

How it works

  1. Split figures into panels. Labels are removed, then each figure is cut along its white gutters. Each panel is classed as an image (microscopy, blots) or a graphic (charts, diagrams). Only images are compared.
  2. Compare every pair of image panels. Distinctive points are matched between the two panels (SIFT features). A geometric check keeps only matches that agree on one rotation, mirroring and resizing.
  3. Check the pixels. The second panel is warped onto the first, and the overlapping pixels are compared. Copies agree on fine grain and noise, not just on shapes. Two different cells that look alike do not.
  4. Report. Each finding shows the two regions side by side, labelled with what kind of reuse it looks like, for a person to judge.

How well it works on papers with known answers

Real manipulated papers do not come with an answer key, so the first test uses synthetic papers. Each one has 2 to 3 figures built from real microscopy images (the Data Science Bowl 2018 nuclei set and scikit-image samples), generated western blots and charts. Figures are saved as compressed JPEG, and half are shrunk, as happens in a real PDF. Some papers get planted reuse. Most also get look-alikes: two different regions of the same sample, which look similar but share no pixels. Those are the cases a careless detector flags.

Thresholds were chosen on 200 development papers, with a rule that at most 5% of clean papers may be flagged. Wrongly flagging an honest paper is the costly mistake. The numbers below are from 200 separate test papers that played no part in tuning. Of those, 70 have planted reuse between panels and 99 have no reuse at all. Another 14 ended up with a real duplicate the generator did not mean to make (two random crops of one image that overlap). They are scored apart from both groups, and the detector flagged 6 of them. The remaining 17 only have a region pasted within one panel, which the default search does not look for.

Counts are small, so a few cases move each rate a lot. On the development papers the same thresholds found 57 of 84 and flagged 2 of 89 clean papers. The threshold search was widened once after an independent review, and its correlation floor is 0.5 because below that a reviewer cannot see the match. With a floor of 0.3 the search would pick 0.4, which finds 58 of 84 on the development papers and, on the test papers, 63 of 80 while flagging 4 of 99 clean papers.

Where it falls short

  • Mostly dark images. All 22 missed reuses were microscopy panels in which at most 11.1% of pixels rose above the dark background. A field with a few nuclei on black has little to match, and after JPEG compression the background is mostly noise.
  • Small overlaps. Two crops that share a strip of their area are the hardest case (3 of 8 found).
  • Blots with a similar layout. The one false alarm in the test was two different western blots with a similar two-band layout, matched after turning one upside down and mirroring it (pixel correlation 0.57). Blots are simple patterns, so a reviewer should look closely at any blot finding with a modest score.
  • Copies within one panel are not searched by default. The tool can look for a region pasted elsewhere in the same image, but on the benchmark it could not tell real pastes from natural repetition (similar nuclei, evenly spaced blot lanes). At its strictest it found 3 of 35 pastes and still flagged 5.1% of clean papers. At its loosest it found 21 of 35 and flagged 100%. It is available as an experimental option.
  • Synthetic papers are not real papers. Real figures have insets, arrows, scale bars, composite colour images and stranger layouts. The retracted-paper check below is the test on real figures.

Papers retracted for image problems

The second test uses real papers. The Retraction Watch database lists neuroscience papers retracted because of duplicated or manipulated images. The check downloads their figures from PubMed Central's open-access collection and runs the detector on them. As a comparison, it also runs on papers from the same journal and year that were never retracted. Those are reported only as counts and are never named.

Using it

pip install git+https://github.com/pearlfranz20/dupscope
dupscope scan fig1.png fig2.png fig3.png --out report/

Open report/report.html. Every finding is a lead for a person to check, not a verdict. Benchmark thresholds: at least 12 geometrically consistent matched points, pixel correlation of at least 0.5, and at least 10% of a panel overlapping. Code, benchmark generator and tests are on GitHub.
